# Conceptual Habitat Module: Preliminary Design Basis

**Status:** Design-basis brief for feasibility work only. This is not a fabrication drawing, structural design, or installation specification.

## 1. Concept

Investigate a small, modular habitat treatment that adds biologically useful surface complexity to an existing breakwater **without cutting into, destabilizing, or changing the protective profile of the breakwater unless separately analyzed and approved**.

The first comparison should be:
- **Control:** existing breakwater surface with no intervention.
- **Treatment A:** habitat texture/relief that does not require penetrating the existing structure, if a qualified engineer determines a safe, stable method is feasible.
- **Treatment B:** a mechanically retained or otherwise engineered module only if the asset owner approves the attachment concept and structural analysis demonstrates acceptable loads and failure behavior.

A module should not be assumed safer merely because it is small or removable. Its weight, hydrodynamic drag, uplift, impact, fatigue, debris retention, and failure mode still require analysis.

## 2. Functional requirements

The module concept should be evaluated against the following requirements before any geometry is frozen.

### Structural and coastal
- No unacceptable reduction in breakwater stability, armor interlock, toe stability, crest function, or maintenance access.
- No unverified drilling, coring, welding, adhesive anchoring, or penetration of armor/core layers.
- No loose components or credible failure mode that creates a navigation hazard.
- Loads assessed for waves, currents, buoyancy, uplift, impact, abrasion, fatigue, debris, corrosion, and installation/maintenance operations.
- Effects on wave transmission, reflection, overtopping, local turbulence, scour, and sediment transport assessed rather than presumed negligible.
- A documented inspection interval, storm-triggered inspection protocol, retrieval plan, and end-of-life removal method.

### Ecological
- Habitat geometry should be selected from local species and life-stage evidence, not copied directly from a tropical coral design.
- Evaluate a range of surface textures, cavities, crevices, and water-exchange pathways; avoid creating dead-end traps for sediment or debris.
- Ensure cavities do not trap organisms in conditions that cause desiccation, overheating, hypoxia, or predation concentration.
- Use marine-compatible, durable, non-leaching materials with documented composition and environmental compatibility.
- Avoid antifouling coatings, biocides, or unverified recycled materials that may release harmful substances.
- Do not introduce non-native species or move organisms between sites without appropriate authorization.
- Monitor whether the treatment adds ecological value relative to nearby untreated structure, rather than merely redistributing organisms.

### Operations and monitoring
- Every component should have a unique ID and documented material, mass, dimensions, installation method, and inspection history.
- Design for safe handling and retrieval with the equipment available at the approved site.
- Ensure the module can be photographed and surveyed repeatedly from consistent positions.
- Include reference/control surfaces and record baseline conditions before installation.

## 3. Material options to screen

No material is selected at this stage. Compare candidate materials using documented evidence for marine durability, leaching, abrasion, repairability, end-of-life handling, and lifecycle cost.

| Material family | Potential benefit | Key checks |
|---|---|---|
| Marine-grade precast concrete | Familiar marine construction material; geometry and surface finish can be controlled | Mix design, curing, cracking, abrasion, alkalinity/leaching, mass, attachment, and compatibility with the host structure |
| Natural rock or quarried stone | Can provide durable rough surface and crevices | Source and gradation, stability, placement impacts, sediment movement, and whether it is actually compatible with a retrofit rather than a separate reef |
| Ceramic/mineral habitat elements | Can provide controlled texture and pore/void geometry | Brittleness, freeze/thermal/shock durability as applicable, abrasion, leaching, attachment, and manufacturability |
| Polymer or composite components | May reduce mass or enable complex shapes | UV and marine aging, fragmentation, fire/chemical history where relevant, leaching, buoyancy, fatigue, and debris risk |

Do not use a material's marketing label (“eco-concrete,” “reef-safe,” “recycled,” or similar) as evidence of suitability. Require material test data and independent review.

## 4. Geometry: design variables, not dimensions

The first prototype should use a parameterized geometry rather than a fixed production size. Variables to explore include:
- external relief and surface roughness;
- cavity opening, depth, and orientation;
- void ratio and through-flow;
- edge radii and snagging risk;
- sediment and debris retention;
- module mass and center of gravity;
- attachment interface and secondary retention;
- inspectability and recovery access.

Dimensions should be set only after target organisms, local water depth, wave/current conditions, structural load limits, manufacturing method, and handling constraints are known. The Okinawa study is a useful precedent for habitat-oriented breakwater design, but its tropical coral outcomes and dimensions cannot be treated as a California design standard.

## 5. Attachment and failure analysis

The preferred order of investigation is:
1. Determine whether a habitat surface treatment can be achieved without penetrating or changing the host structure.
2. If not, evaluate an attachment concept with the asset owner and a qualified coastal/structural engineer.
3. Model the complete load path from the module through the attachment and armor to the underlying structure.
4. Analyze single-component loss, partial detachment, impact, progressive failure, and debris hazards.
5. Confirm inspectability, safe retrieval, corrosion/aging behavior, and post-storm response.
6. Reject any concept whose safety depends on an unverified assumption about the host structure.

No anchor type, bolt size, adhesive, drilling depth, fastener spacing, or allowable load is specified here. Those values require the actual breakwater drawings, material condition, load cases, and engineer-of-record approval.

## 6. Prototype and test sequence

### Stage 0 - Desk study
Define target habitat function, gather local species and environmental data, establish constraints, and develop candidate geometries.

### Stage 1 - Material and geometry coupons
Test small surface coupons or non-deployed specimens for durability, leaching, surface texture, and manufacturability. Biological tests require appropriate permits and protocols.

### Stage 2 - Laboratory / numerical assessment
Use qualified engineering analysis to evaluate flow, drag, uplift, wave interaction, and attachment loads. Physical modeling may be warranted if numerical methods cannot adequately resolve the relevant processes.

### Stage 3 - Non-structural field trial, if authorized
If a suitable permitted site exists, consider a small, retrievable test away from critical infrastructure to evaluate ecological colonization and material durability before modifying the breakwater. This is a separate option, not automatic approval to place material offshore.

### Stage 4 - Breakwater pilot, only after gates pass
Install a limited number of uniquely identified modules with matched controls, baseline surveys, a monitoring plan, storm-triggered inspection, and a funded removal contingency.

## 7. Minimum monitoring plan

Collect baseline data before treatment, then sample on a schedule appropriate to seasonal ecology and storm exposure. A qualified ecology team should set species-specific methods and timing.

- Structural: position, movement, cracking, corrosion, attachment condition, loss, and post-storm damage.
- Physical: local flow/wave conditions as warranted, scour, sediment accumulation, and module stability.
- Water quality: temperature, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, and other site-specific variables.
- Biological: species composition, native versus non-native colonization, abundance/cover, size classes, and habitat use.
- Comparison: matched untreated surfaces and, where feasible, before-after/control-impact analysis.
- Data quality: fixed photo points, metadata, calibration, replicates, and transparent reporting of uncertainty.

Set quantitative success thresholds only after baseline variability, target ecological function, and safety limits are established. A pilot should stop or be removed if predefined structural, navigation, environmental, or ecological stop-work criteria are triggered.

## 8. Decisions required before a dimensioned drawing

- Which native habitat function is the priority: intertidal refuge, submerged hard substrate, kelp-associated habitat, or another locally supported objective?
- Which candidate segment is authorized for study, and what are its surveyed cross-section and local environmental loads?
- What is the host structure's verified condition and permissible additional load?
- What is the acceptable failure consequence and recovery method?
- Which materials have suitable independent test data?
- What approvals, consultations, and permits are required?
- Who funds, inspects, maintains, and ultimately removes the pilot?

## 9. Evidence basis and limitations

The concept is informed by the 2021 Okinawa hybrid-breakwater study by Tanaya and colleagues, but it does not transfer the study's coral outcomes to Southern California. Regional planning should begin with the USACE East San Pedro Bay Ecosystem Restoration study and current local survey data.

- Tanaya, T., Kinjo, N., Okada, W., Yasuda, M., & Kuwae, T. (2021). “Improvement of the coral growth and cost-effectiveness of hybrid infrastructure by an innovative breakwater design in Naha Port, Okinawa, Japan.” *Coastal Engineering Journal*, 63(3), 248–262. [https://doi.org/10.1080/21664250.2021.1889823](https://doi.org/10.1080/21664250.2021.1889823)
- [USACE East San Pedro Bay Ecosystem Restoration Study](https://www.spl.usace.army.mil/Missions/Civil-Works/Projects-and-Studies/East-San-Pedro-Bay-Ecosystem-Restoration-Study/)
