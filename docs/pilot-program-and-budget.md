# Pilot Program and Budget Framework

## Objective

Test whether a limited habitat-enhancing retrofit can improve native habitat outcomes while preserving the existing breakwater's structural and hydraulic performance.

## Proposed stages

### Stage 1 — Records and baseline (planning allowance: 3–6 months)
- Obtain as-built drawings, inspection and maintenance records, bathymetry, wave/current data, and existing restoration-study appendices.
- Conduct targeted structural, ecological, and sediment surveys where records are incomplete.
- Identify candidate locations and a comparable reference site.

### Stage 2 — Concept development and screening (4–8 months)
- Develop several module geometries and attachment concepts.
- Screen material compatibility, structural loading, constructability, habitat function, and maintenance.
- Compare baseline, habitat-only, and habitat-plus-reef alternatives.
- Decide whether physical modeling is required.

### Stage 3 — Detailed pilot design and approvals (6–18+ months)
- Complete engineering design, environmental documentation, stakeholder coordination, and permitting.
- Specify installation method, quality assurance, inspection access, contingency plans, and removal procedures.
- Secure funds for installation and the full monitoring period before construction.

### Stage 4 — Installation and initial monitoring (6–12 months)
- Install a limited pilot only after required approvals.
- Document as-built condition and establish repeatable structural and biological measurements.
- Monitor during multiple conditions and seasons; adjust frequency based on risk and regulator requirements.

### Stage 5 — Long-term evaluation (2–5 years)
- Compare biological outcomes with baseline and control sites.
- Inspect module stability, scour, maintenance needs, and hydraulic indicators.
- Publish findings, uncertainty, and lessons learned before deciding whether to expand.

These are preliminary planning allowances, not a committed schedule. Stages may overlap, and permitting, seasonal restrictions, procurement, and access may extend them.

## Budget structure

Do not publish a single dollar estimate until quantities, access, and site conditions are defined. Prepare a transparent estimate with separate line items for:

1. Records review, survey, and data acquisition
2. Coastal, structural, geotechnical, and ecological engineering
3. Numerical and physical modeling
4. Module design, tooling, fabrication, and material testing
5. Environmental documentation and permits
6. Port coordination, vessel/barge, crane, and installation
7. Construction monitoring and quality assurance
8. Baseline and multi-year ecological monitoring
9. Inspection, repair, replacement, and removal contingency
10. Project management, escalation, and contingency

For each line item, record the quantity basis, unit cost source, estimate date, uncertainty range, and exclusions. Distinguish one-time pilot costs from recurring lifecycle costs. Compare the pilot against a conventional reef-restoration alternative and the existing maintenance baseline.

## Pilot acceptance criteria

Before installation, the project team should establish numeric criteria supported by engineering analysis and baseline data for:
- Module and armor stability
- Scour and sediment response
- Wave transmission and overtopping
- Navigation and maintenance access
- Water quality and ecological disturbance
- Native habitat development relative to controls
- Lifecycle cost and maintenance effort

Expand only if safety and operational criteria are met and the measured ecological benefit justifies lifecycle cost. If the pilot fails, implement the preapproved repair, modification, or removal plan.
