# Evidence Review: Okinawa Precedent and Long Beach Transferability

**Status:** Desk review of published research and official project records  
**Purpose:** Separate demonstrated findings from design hypotheses for a potential Long Beach retrofit.  
**Not a design approval:** This document does not establish site suitability, structural adequacy, permit eligibility, or expected performance in California.

## Executive summary

The Okinawa precedent is a useful proof of concept for integrating habitat features into breakwaters. It is not proof that the same module geometry, species response, wave performance, or cost-effectiveness will transfer to Long Beach.

A peer-reviewed 2021 study by Tanaya et al. examined pro-environment breakwaters at Naha Port, Okinawa, including artificial tide pools (ATPs) at different depths. The paper reported greater coral area than on a normal control breakwater: approximately 10% and 20% higher in its abstract for shallow and deeper ATP configurations, respectively. The detailed results report coral area per meter of breakwater extension of 10.15 m² for the control, 11.53 m² for the shallow configuration, and 12.41 m² for the deeper configuration. Construction cost per meter was also higher for the modified designs; the deeper configuration's coral-area-per-construction-cost metric was approximately 10% above the control.

The Long Beach project must treat those figures as **site-specific evidence from a tropical coral environment**, not as forecasts for California. The transferable lesson is the research method: compare modified and conventional infrastructure, measure habitat outcomes, account for construction costs, and test design choices against local physical and ecological conditions.

## 1. What the Okinawa study demonstrated

**Primary publication**

Tanaya, T., Kinjo, N., Okada, W., Yasuda, M., & Kuwae, T. (2021). “Improvement of the coral growth and cost-effectiveness of hybrid infrastructure by an innovative breakwater design in Naha Port, Okinawa, Japan.” *Coastal Engineering Journal*, 63(3), 248–262. DOI: [10.1080/21664250.2021.1889823](https://doi.org/10.1080/21664250.2021.1889823).

- The study compared a normal control breakwater with pro-environment breakwater designs containing artificial tide pools at shallow or deeper locations.
- Its ecological effectiveness metric was coral-covered area on the breakwaters.
- The abstract reports approximately 10% more coral area for the shallow design and 20% more for the deeper design versus control. The detailed results report approximately 14% and 22% higher coral area per meter, respectively.
- The deeper design had approximately 10% higher coral-area-per-construction-cost effectiveness than the control in the study's cost accounting.
- The study's outcome is about coral habitat area and a defined construction-cost ratio. It does **not** establish that every habitat retrofit improves wave attenuation, reduces flooding, or lowers lifecycle cost.

## 2. What transfers to Long Beach, and what does not

| Design lesson from Okinawa | Application to Long Beach | Validation needed |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat geometry can change biological colonization | Evaluate textured modules, cavities, and varied surface relief | Local colonization trials and reference sites |
| Depth and placement matter | Design zones based on local depth, light, exposure, and flow | Bathymetry, wave/current data, ecological survey |
| Habitat benefits should be measured against a control | Use an untreated or conventional-surface comparison site | Baseline sampling plan and statistical design |
| Construction cost should be considered alongside ecological outcome | Estimate cost per unit of measured habitat function and lifecycle cost | Quantities, installation costs, maintenance and replacement |
| Breakwaters can have both protective and ecological functions | Retain protection as a design constraint while testing habitat additions | Structural and hydrodynamic modeling |

**Do not transfer directly:** Okinawan coral growth rates, coral-specific cavity dimensions, the reported percentage gains, tropical species assumptions, or the study's cost-effectiveness result.

For Long Beach, candidate ecological outcomes should be selected with Southern California marine ecologists. Depending on local conditions, these could include native sessile invertebrate cover, native algae, species richness, recruitment, fish use, and the condition of nearby kelp or rocky-reef communities. These are proposed indicators, not guaranteed benefits.

## 3. The Long Beach baseline already studied

The USACE and City of Long Beach prepared the *East San Pedro Bay Ecosystem Restoration Study — Final Integrated Feasibility Report / Environmental Impact Statement / Environmental Impact Report*. The study area covers approximately 18 square miles of East San Pedro Bay and evaluated restoration alternatives including rocky-reef habitat that could support kelp, eelgrass, and other sensitive species or habitat types.

The USACE project page identifies Alternative 4A as the Recommended National Ecosystem Restoration Plan. The USACE April 16, 2023 fact sheet records financial closeout in FY2023 and no planned accomplishments for FY2024. This means the historical feasibility work is an important reference, but its recommendation should not be described as an active, funded construction project without current confirmation.

### How this proposal differs

The proposed retrofit asks whether habitat features can be integrated into selected existing breakwater surfaces while retaining the existing protective profile. The earlier ecosystem study evaluated broader restoration alternatives, including new reef habitat and breakwater-modification options. The retrofit is therefore a **distinct hypothesis to evaluate against the existing alternatives**, not a claim that prior work overlooked habitat restoration or that a retrofit is automatically cheaper or better.

Before proposing a pilot, review the final report and technical appendices for the selected alternative, hydraulic and sediment findings, environmental effects, estimated quantities, and the reasons particular alternatives were advanced or rejected.

## 4. Research questions that remain open

1. Which segments of the existing breakwater have suitable structural condition, access, depth, and exposure for a retrofit?
2. What are the existing armor configuration, foundation conditions, design wave criteria, and inspection history?
3. Would habitat modules affect wave transmission, overtopping, armor stability, local scour, or maintenance?
4. Which native communities are appropriate targets at each candidate depth and exposure?
5. What existing habitat would be disturbed by installation, and could the project introduce invasive-species or water-quality risks?
6. What permits, owner approvals, navigation coordination, and environmental review would be required?
7. What is the full lifecycle cost per unit of ecological benefit compared with new reef placement or no action?
8. What measurable result would justify expansion, and what failure threshold would require removal?

## 5. Recommended pilot study design

- **Baseline:** document existing structure, habitat, bathymetry, water quality, and relevant wave/current conditions.
- **Treatments:** compare the existing surface with one or more habitat-module configurations. Include a reef-toe option only if preliminary engineering supports it.
- **Controls:** select matched reference locations and use a before-after/control-impact design where feasible.
- **Measurements:** monitor native biological cover and composition, recruitment, sediment accumulation, water quality, module movement, scour, and relevant hydraulic indicators.
- **Duration:** monitor across multiple seasons; establish the final duration with ecologists, engineers, and permitting agencies.
- **Decision rule:** expand only if safety and operational requirements are met and the measured ecological benefit is meaningful relative to cost and control sites.

A small pilot should not be used to claim improved tsunami protection or reduced coastal flooding unless separate modeling and evidence support those outcomes.

## Conclusion

Okinawa demonstrates that carefully designed habitat features can increase coral habitat on a breakwater under the conditions studied. Long Beach offers a different ecology, wave climate, structure, and planning history. The defensible next step is a locally calibrated feasibility comparison and controlled pilot, with coastal protection treated as a non-negotiable constraint and ecological gains measured rather than assumed.
